Seniority in engineering is not defined by years of service — it is defined by how much the team is better because you are in it. This lesson covers both halves of that equation: how to mentor and grow junior engineers (the force multiplier most teams lack), and how to keep your own growth compounding after the day-to-day novelty wears off.

## 1. Mentoring — What It Actually Is (and Isn't)

Mentoring is not handing over answers. It is building the mentee's ability to find them. The difference:

| Dumping answers | Mentoring |
| :-- | :-- |
| "The bug is on line 42." | "Walk me through what the logs show at the call site. What does the 500 body contain?" |
| "Use Redis for this." | "What are our options for shared state here? What breaks at 10× traffic?" |
| "Here's my PR, rubber-stamp it." | Review their PR *with* them: ask questions, let them spot the issue |

The mentoring loop: **diagnose → calibrate → stretch → reflect**. Diagnose the current level (reading, tracing, designing, judging), calibrate tasks just above it (that's where growth lives), stretch gradually, and debrief every sticking point.

### The 1-on-1 that works (30 minutes, weekly)

1. **5 min** — mentee drives: what shipped, what's stuck, what's unclear.
2. **15 min** — one deep item only. Not a status list. One hard thing, fully explored.
3. **5 min** — one growth action for the week (shadow a review, read an ADR, write one doc paragraph).
4. **5 min** — blockers you can clear as the senior person (access, intro, context).

Track it: a shared doc per mentee with goals, shipped items, feedback themes. Memory lies; the doc doesn't.

## 2. Giving Feedback That Lands

Feedback fails when it is vague, delayed, or personal. Use the format that survives on all three axes:

> **Situation** ("In the payments RFC review yesterday") → **Behavior** ("the idempotency section had no failure-mode analysis") → **Impact** ("the reviewer can't assess retry safety; it will surface in code review as rework") → **Request** ("add one section on retries — pattern is in `docs/adr/013`").

And catch the reverse direction too — junior engineers praise specifically, not generically:

- "Good commit message" is noise. "The commit message explained *why* the backoff cap is 30s — that's what let the on-call fix it at 3am" is a signal they can repeat.

**Praise publicly, correct privately, escalate only patterns — never incidents.** One missed thing is coaching; a repeated miss is a performance conversation with their manager, and that decision is made after you've tried feedback twice.

## 3. Delegation That Doesn't Backfire

The senior trap: "I could do it in 20 minutes vs training them for two hours." Every time you take the 20-minute task, you reconfirm you're the bottleneck. Delegate deliberately:

- **Scope the risk**: hand off fully-reversible work (tests, docs, non-critical endpoints), not migrations or auth.
- **Define done**, then stay out. Check in at agreed checkpoints, not on a whim.
- **Let them stumble small**: a broken staging branch is the cheapest education they'll ever get — far better than a hotfix at 2am on a payments bug.
- **Review the work, not the person**: "the migration needs an index on user_id" not "you forgot the index again".

## 4. Your Own Continuous Growth

Post-courseness, growth comes from deliberate practice, not exposure. Three levers that compound:

1. **Depth in one axis per quarter.** Pick one: databases, distributed systems, security, observability, or the domain (payments, logistics, whatever your product is). Produce one artifact per axis (a talk, a doc, a PR, a postmortem write-up).
2. **Deliberate discomfort.** Volunteer for the incident, the on-call, the hairy migration, the design review nobody wants. Growth is load-bearing pain, applied intentionally.
3. **Write more than you read.** Each week: one paragraph posted in the team channel about something you learned. Forces precision; builds your reputation; creates the corpus of your own thinking.

### The senior engineer's reading list skeleton

- One system of record per year (e.g., *Designing Data-Intensive Applications*, *Site Reliability Engineering*, or your domain's equivalent)
- ADRs and RFCs from your own org and three admired external ones (e.g., Stripe, Cloudflare engineering blogs)
- Postmortems — internal and public; the failure patterns repeat
- Release notes of one tool you use daily — nothing in 2026 stays static

## 5. Teaching Down, Learning Up, and Sideways

- **Down** (mentees): per sections 1–3.
- **Up** (staff/principal): bring them working proposals with options, not open-ended problems. The fastest way to be trusted senior is to make the higher-up's decision trivial.
- **Sideways** (peers): share what you know aggressively — internal talks, brown-bags, pairing. Invisible work doesn't promote; visible expertise does.

## 6. Career Growth Conversations

- Know your own promotion criteria (ask your manager explicitly) and map your last 6 months against it — evidence, not feelings.
- Ask for the gap analysis quarterly: "What's between me and the next level, concretely?"
- If there is no path, build one — a new scope, a new domain, an adjacent team's problem.
- **Switch jobs on the curve, not at the peak**: skill growth, not title, compounds. A promotion that stops your learning is a pay cut in disguise.

## 7. Exercise

Set up a structured 4-week growth sprint for yourself or a mentee: pick one axis, one artifact, two stretch tasks, and a Friday 30-minute reflection slot. Write the plan, execute week 1, and adjust.

**Acceptance criteria.** Your plan contains: chosen axis, artifact, two tasks that stretch without snapping, a reflection slot on the calendar, and one honest sentence about where you're actually weak.
